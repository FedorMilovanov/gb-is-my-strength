Chapter 10 owns the **birth and early restructuring of the Soviet legal procedure, 1918–1921**.

## 1. 1918 baseline — separation decree, article 6

The separation decree establishes a general rule:
- religious belief does not automatically excuse a citizen from civil obligations;
- substitutions from one civil duty to another may be allowed in individual cases by a people's court.

This is the legal bridge to the later specific military-service decrees.

Safe synthesis:
> The new Soviet order did not recognize a blanket religious exemption from civic duty, but from the beginning left a judicial route for substitution in individual cases.

Do not write:
- `the 1918 decree granted pacifists complete exemption`;
- `the 1918 decree abolished conscientious objection`.

Status: **PRIMARY TEXT CLOSED**.

## 2. 4 Jan 1919 — first specific Soviet conscientious-objection procedure

Primary decree, S.U. 1919 №17 art.192.

### Rights created

A person unable by religious conviction to participate in military service could ask a People's Court to substitute:
- sanitary service, preferably in infectious hospitals; or
- other socially useful work.

### Mandatory expertise

The court had to request an expert opinion from the Moscow `Объединенный Совет Религиозных общин и групп` (OSROG) in every individual case.

The expertise had two tasks:
1. determine whether the religious doctrine genuinely excluded military participation;
2. determine whether the individual claimant sincerely and consistently lived according to that doctrine.

### Full exemption

In exceptional cases OSROG, by unanimous decision, could petition the VTsIK Presidium for complete exemption without substitution.

### Production-safe interpretation

> The state recognized religious conscientious objection, but converted it into an evidentiary judicial procedure. The claimant's doctrine and personal sincerity became objects of expert examination.

This is accommodation **and** administrative scrutiny at the same time.

Do not reduce the decree to either `religious freedom` or `repression` alone.

Status: **PRIMARY LEGAL CLAIM CLOSED**.

---

## 3. Enforcement tightening / evidentiary scrutiny in 1919

A later NKYu instruction on applying the 4 Jan decree insisted that expertise be substantive rather than formal. OSROG had to establish the claimant's actual life and conduct, not merely membership in a religious group.

Editorial consequence:
- the procedure was not denominational automaticity;
- being Baptist/Evangelical Christian did not by itself guarantee exemption;
- sincerity and biography were state-legible evidence.

This matters for the later role of church leaders such as Y. I. Zhidkov as religious experts.

Status: **PRIMARY ADMINISTRATIVE CONTROL STRONG**.

## 4. 14 Dec 1920 — procedure decentralized from mandatory OSROG monopoly

Primary decree, S.U. 1920 №99 art.527.

### What remained

- substitution for military service remained legally available;
- sanitary or other socially useful work remained the default substitute;
- complete exemption remained possible in exceptional cases.

### What changed

The court now relied on:
- witness testimony and evidence about the claimant's life;
- expert conclusions about doctrine and the claimant's adherence to it.

Instead of mandatory OSROG expertise in every case, the court could invite trusted knowledgeable representatives of the relevant religious teaching, preferably local persons familiar with the community and its way of life.

The decree also imposed procedure:
- application at least one week before call-up;
- court certificate for a timely applicant;
- no detention before the court decision for such timely applicants.

### Safe interpretation

> In December 1920 the state retained conscientious objection as a judicially administered right but changed the expert mechanism from a Moscow-centered mandatory OSROG channel toward a more localized court-controlled evidentiary process.

Do not write `OSROG was abolished by this decree` unless a separate institutional source proves abolition. The closed claim is narrower: **its mandatory expert monopoly in this procedure disappeared**.

Status: **PRIMARY LEGAL CLAIM CLOSED**.

## 5. 16 Sep 1921 — retrospective review of convictions

Primary VTsIK decree ordered review of **all** cases of persons convicted for evasion of military duty or desertion on religious grounds, including convictions imposed by:
- judicial organs;
- commissions for combating desertion;
- Extraordinary Commissions (Cheka).

### Review mechanism

Special judicial sessions were to re-examine evidence under the 14 Dec 1920 standard.

If sincerity was established:
- a person could be released from military duty when the relevant age cohort had already been demobilized; or
- imprisonment could be replaced with work permitted under the conscientious-objection framework, including agricultural or other work by specialty when peers remained in the Red Army.

Document date: **16 Sep 1921**.  
Publication: `Известия ВЦИК` №208, **18 Sep 1921**.

### Why this matters

This decree proves two things at once:
1. the state still recognized a formal legal category of religious conscientious objection in 1921;
2. enough people had already been punished by courts, anti-desertion commissions and Cheka bodies that a general review order became necessary.

Safe synthesis:
> By 1921 the conscientious-objection regime was neither a paper freedom nor a simple ban. It was a contested legal field in which recognition, punishment, evidentiary scrutiny and corrective review coexisted.

This is one of the chapter's strongest examples of the broader thesis that early Soviet religious policy was legally structured but unevenly enforced.

Status: **PRIMARY LEGAL CLAIM CLOSED**.

## 7. Relation to Tver 1921

The Tver youth-congress arrests and the conscientious-objection legal chain belong in the same chapter but are not one case.

Do not imply the Tver arrests were caused by military refusal unless an archival document says so.

Their analytical relationship is structural:
- both show uneven relations between legal permission, local coercion and central review/intervention;
- both demonstrate why `central policy` and `local enforcement` must be separated.

## 8. Chapter 10 narrative use

Recommended progression:

1. 1917 open public field and discussion of political/religious freedom.
2. Jan 1918 separation decree: equality + loss of corporate legal/property status.
3. Jan 1919 military-conscience procedure: legal recognition + expert scrutiny.
4. Dec 1920 reform: localized judicial expertise + procedural safeguards.
5. 1921 review decree: correction of existing punitive outcomes.
6. Tver 1921: another example of permission/coercion/central correction outside the military-service question.

This progression supports the chapter thesis without pretending Soviet policy was stable, uniform or benevolent.
