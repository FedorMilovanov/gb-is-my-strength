## 0. Why this file exists

Georgi Vins is the strongest human bridge between Chapters 19 and 20.

But his 1979 release/exile is vulnerable to two opposite simplifications:

> `Western Christian pressure freed Vins.`

and

> `The West had little to do with it; the Soviet state simply chose to expel him.`

The surviving U.S. official record now permits a more precise reconstruction:

```text
international church/public advocacy
       ↓
formal congressional action by 1976
       ↓
human-rights case becomes politically visible
       ↓
U.S.–Soviet backchannel discussion of prisoner exchange in 1978
       ↓
Vins explicitly enters proposed exchange package
       ↓
April 1979 agreement on named five-for-two exchange
       ↓
27 Apr 1979 release/expulsion/arrival
       ↓
negotiated family-departure provisions
       ↓
Vins becomes a direct witness in U.S. public/government forums
```

This establishes a real international-political causal environment without claiming that one petition, church campaign or congressional resolution mechanically produced release.

# A. 1976 — VINS IS ALREADY A FORMAL U.S. CONGRESSIONAL CASE

## 1. House Report 94-1464

Primary U.S. government object:
- 94th Congress, 2nd Session;
- House Report **No. 94-1464**;
- title: `Religious Repression in the Soviet Union: Dissident Baptist Pastor Georgi Vins`;
- **2 Sep 1976**;
- to accompany H. Con. Res. 726;
- U.S. Government Printing Office / Serial Set scan.

Official GovInfo PDF:
- `https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-13134_11_00-020-1464-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-13134_11_00-020-1464-0000.pdf`

Physical/digital extent currently exposed:
- 8 scan pages in GovInfo object.

### Exact page 1 controls

The first page states:
- H. Con. Res. 726 urged the Soviet Union to release Vins and permit religious believers to worship according to conscience;
- original congressional resolution introduced **6 Apr 1976** with 24 cosponsors;
- subsequent related resolutions were used to add sponsors/cosponsors;
- by report time total sponsors/cosponsors = **140**;
- joint subcommittee hearings held **24 Jun and 30 Jun 1976**.

### Witnesses named by the report

24 Jun:
- Rev. Janis Smits;
- Rev. David Klassen;
- Bohdan R. Bociurkiw.

30 Jun:
- Rep. John H. Buchanan Jr.;
- John Dunlop;
- Lev Dobriansky;
- George Dobczansky;
- plus written statements including Haralan Popov and American-Latvian Association.

### Evidence verdict

Closed:
> `By September 1976 the Vins case had become a formal congressional religious-freedom case with 140 sponsors/cosponsors and two days of hearings.`

Not closed by this report:
> `Congressional action caused Vins's release three years later.`

## 2. Report 94-1464 itself records transnational campaign scale — as attributed evidence

The report summarizes Buchanan's testimony citing Rev. David Hathaway regarding an international campaign:
- demonstrations/petitions in 13 countries;
- reportedly more than 600,000 petition names presented to Soviet embassies;
- reported marches/rallies in London, Dublin, Bonn and West Berlin;
- British parliamentary motion `Right to Believe`.

### Critical numeric control

These figures are **reported inside a congressional report from a witness's source**.

They are not automatically independent U.S. government counts.

Production use:
> `По приведенным в докладе Конгресса данным международной кампании...`

not:
> `600,000 people are independently verified by Congress.`

# B. THE CASE WAS ALSO RAISED OUTSIDE THE UNITED STATES

## 3. UK Parliament — official government response, 11 Jul 1977

Hansard records a written question by Rev. Ian Paisley asking the Foreign Secretary whether the UK government would make representations to Soviet authorities for Vins's release under Helsinki principles.

Official answer by Mr. Luard:
- British government had taken steps to ensure Soviet authorities knew of concern about the case;
- called the concern legitimate;
- said the case would continue to be considered in private discussions and at the Belgrade CSCE review meeting.

Public Hansard:
- `https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/1977-07-11/debates/0665e7fa-e2f2-4001-be94-b4939fbc3307/WrittenAnswers`

### Meaning

This proves:
> the Vins case had entered official British diplomatic/human-rights discussion by 1977.

It does not prove:
> British representations caused the 1979 exchange.

# C. 1978 — PRISONER EXCHANGE DISCUSSION ENTERS THE OFFICIAL RECORD

## 4. Brzezinski–Dobrynin discussion, 1978

Foreign Relations of the United States (FRUS), 1977–1980, vol. VI preserves a memorandum in which Soviet Ambassador Anatoliy Dobrynin discussed possible exchange of the two Soviet espionage defendants for Soviet dissident/religious cases.

Official State Department historical record:
- FRUS vol. VI, Document 146 / note context;
- page reference around p.458 in published volume.

The record says Dobrynin:
- ruled out Shcharanskiy as too symbolically charged for discussion;
- suggested release of the two Soviets in exchange for less notorious cases;
- gave **`the Vins family`** as an example.

Brzezinski responded that obscure cases would not give the exchange the desired positive political effect and that publicly prominent cases were needed.

Official source:
- `https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v06/d146fn1`

### Analytical value

This is the earliest current direct negotiation-level source showing Vins in a possible U.S.–Soviet exchange conversation.

It proves:
> Vins was not merely added at the last minute in April 1979.

It still does not prove:
> a specific church petition caused his inclusion.

# D. APRIL 1979 — NAMED EXCHANGE PACKAGE BECOMES EXPLICIT

## 5. Brzezinski–Dobrynin meeting, 23 Apr 1979

FRUS Document 188 note/context preserves the 23 Apr conversation.

Participants:
- Anatoliy Dobrynin;
- Zbigniew Brzezinski.

Dobrynin said Soviet authorities were prepared by 1 May to expel from the USSR:
- **Georgi Vins**;
- Valentyn Moroz;
- Eduard Kuznetsov;
- Mark Dymshits;
- Aleksandr Ginzburg;
- plus immediate family members who wished to accompany them, though some might follow later.

In return the U.S. would expel:
- Rudolf Chernyayev;
- Valdik Enger.

The sides discussed exchange venue and direct Moscow/New York travel.

Official State Department historical source:
- `https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v06/d188fn2`

Footnote says Dobrynin agreed to terms in an **25 Apr** message.

### Evidence verdict

This is strong official evidence for the actual negotiated package.

Safe:
> `By 23 April 1979 Vins was explicitly part of the named U.S.–Soviet exchange package under discussion.`

Unsafe:
> `The U.S. bought Vins's freedom for two spies.`

The documents describe a reciprocal prisoner/expulsion arrangement, not a commercial transaction.

# E. 27 APR 1979 — EXCHANGE EVENT

## 6. Same-day public statement

White House statement, **27 Apr 1979**, establishes public completion:
- President Carter commuted Enger and Chernyayev;
- Soviet government released Dymshits, Ginzburg, Kuznetsov, Moroz and Vins;
- the five arrived in the United States;
- immediate families were expected to follow.

This is the principal public event anchor.

### Terminology

Preferred:
- `обмен заключенными / согласованный обмен`;
- `советская сторона освободила и выслала/передала в рамках обмена` where source/legal status supports;
- `прибыл в США 27 Apr 1979`.

Avoid:
- `добровольно эмигрировал`;
- `был просто освобожден и решил уехать`;
- `США освободили его`.

# F. FAMILY DEPARTURE WAS PART OF THE AGREEMENT, NOT AN AFTERTHOUGHT

## 7. Brzezinski to Carter, 24 May 1979

FRUS Document 195:
- memorandum from Brzezinski to Carter;
- **24 May 1979**;
- subject: `Aftermath of the Prisoner Exchange—Treatment of the Families`.

It explicitly states:
> the U.S.–Soviet agreement specified that immediate family members of the five prisoners would be allowed to leave the Soviet Union `without obstacle or delay`.

The memo then records obstacles imposed on families after the exchange.

For the Vins family specifically:
- Nadezhda Vins was under heavy surveillance;
- Soviet officials said a power of attorney from Georgi Vins could not be recognized because he was no longer a Soviet citizen;
- local officials imposed false departure deadlines;
- Vins's niece initially was denied permission;
- Mrs. Vins said she would not leave without the niece;
- U.S. protests corrected several family-departure problems.

Official source:
- `https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v06/d195`

### High-value implication

This strengthens two fields:

1. **citizenship status** — official U.S. memo records Soviet officials treating Vins as no longer a Soviet citizen;
2. **family departure** — family exit was part of the negotiated package.

### Still open

The memo does not substitute for:
- exact Soviet decree depriving Vins of citizenship;
- exact exit/visa instruments for each family member.

# G. CAUSAL MODEL — WHAT CAN NOW BE SAID

## 8. A stronger but controlled causal formulation

Current documents support:

> **The 1979 exchange followed years in which Vins's case had become an international religious-freedom issue and a subject of parliamentary/congressional action. By 1978 his case was explicitly present in U.S.–Soviet exchange discussions; in April 1979 the sides negotiated a named five-for-two package including Vins and provisions for immediate-family departure.**

This is much stronger than merely saying:
> `Vins was famous in the West.`

But it is still more precise than:
> `international Christian pressure freed Vins.`

Why the latter remains too strong:
- diplomacy included broader strategic/political calculations;
- Vins was exchanged together with four other prominent prisoners;
- Soviet/U.S. intelligence-prisoner issues formed the reciprocal side of the agreement;
- no current source assigns a single decisive causal weight to Baptist petitions vs congressional pressure vs executive diplomacy vs Soviet calculations.

# 16. Current Vins verdict

The 1976–1979 documentary chain now supports a much stronger chapter than either a hagiographic rescue story or a purely diplomatic swap story.

The controlled narrative is:

> **Vins's imprisonment became an international religious-freedom issue years before his release. The case entered parliamentary and congressional channels, then explicit U.S.–Soviet exchange negotiations. By April 1979 Vins was one of five named prisoners in an agreed reciprocal package. The agreement also covered departure of immediate family members. Within weeks of arriving in the United States, Vins himself became a primary public witness before U.S. institutions, and his testimony was then redistributed through Baptist and archival networks.**

This shows the transformation central to Chapter 20:

> **a person who had been the object of international advocacy becomes the producer of international evidence.**
