## 2. XXV congress resolution — what can safely be said now

The best-supported documentary/denominational reconstruction gives this substance:
- civil/state obligations were acknowledged;
- participation in bloodshed was rejected;
- delegates did not reach one common view on the practical form of military duty;
- the method of fulfilling the duty was therefore left to the believer's **personal conscience**.

This is materially different from the later 1926 formula.

Safe chapter synthesis:
> `В 1923 году съезд не принял простую формулу обязательной вооружённой службы: государственные обязанности признавались, но вопрос о способе исполнения воинской повинности при несогласии делегатов оставлялся личной совести верующего.`

Do not quote this sentence as if it were verbatim protocol wording. The exact original XXV protocol/resolution page remains a P0 facsimile target.

## 3. Arrest-count conflict — `11` vs `21` vs `6`

### 3.1 `11` — canonical working total

Multiple independent historical reconstructions give **11** pacifist delegates arrested during or around the closing phase of the congress. This figure is repeated in modern Baptist historical material and in scholarly reconstruction of the military-question conflict.

Editorial status:
- **11 = CANONICAL WORKING NUMBER / STRONG**;
- it may be used in research prose with qualification such as `по наиболее устойчивой документальной линии` until the original arrest list is recovered;
- do not label it `PRIMARY-CLOSED` yet.

### 3.2 `6` — archival Commission-approved key subset, not the total

Archive-based A. I. Savin reconstruction of Anti-Religious Commission records shows a **5 Dec 1923** decision approving the arrest of **six** particularly active delegates, headed by M. D. Timoshenko and I. N. Shilov.

The wider documentary tradition names the six exiled/key pacifist figures as:
- M. D. Timoshenko;
- I. N. Shilov;
- N. A. Levindanto;
- R. D. Khomyak;
- Glagolev;
- Printsev.

Editorial rule:
> `6` must not be substituted for the aggregate arrest total. It is an archival-record subset / explicitly sanctioned group within the broader repression episode.

Status: **SIX-PERSON COMMISSION SUBSET STRONG / EXACT ARCHIVE LIST IMAGE OPEN**.

## 4. Why the arrests matter analytically

The chapter must not treat the arrests as a detachable anecdote.

The sequence is:
1. military question under active state/security scrutiny;
2. explicit contingency plan to split the congress if it rejected military service;
3. arrests of leading pacifist delegates;
4. congress still stops short of the 1926 armed-service formula and leaves practical fulfillment to personal conscience.

This supports a careful conclusion:
> state coercion affected the institutional environment, but the 1923 result cannot be reduced to either total capitulation or complete freedom from pressure.

## 7. The 1926 doctrinal/institutional change

Later official Baptist history and the surviving proceedings citation trail support the core change:
- the congress returned to the military-service formula associated with Article 13 of the 1905/1906 Russian Baptist confession;
- military service when required by government was recognized as compatible with Baptist duty in the central union's official formula.

This differs sharply from the 1923 personal-conscience compromise.

Two truths must be carried together:

### A. Genuine Baptist precedent
The 1905/1906 confession is real pre-Soviet Baptist precedent. Therefore the 1926 formula cannot honestly be described as a completely invented OGPU doctrine with no Baptist antecedent.

### B. Coercive state context
The 1926 congress occurred after years of Anti-Religious Commission/OGPU pressure, arrests of pacifist leaders, legal narrowing of exemption, and state interest in securing a compliant central denominational position.

Therefore the opposite simplification is also forbidden: the vote cannot be narrated as if this coercive environment did not exist.

Safe synthesis:
> `The 1926 decision had a real pre-revolutionary confessional precedent, but it was made inside a political environment deliberately shaped by state pressure against evangelical pacifism.`

## 9. What the 1926 decision does not prove

It does not prove:
- that every Baptist believer abandoned pacifism;
- that every local congregation immediately adopted the same practical position;
- that no theological argument for military duty existed before Soviet pressure;
- that coercion was absent;
- that every supporter voted solely from fear;
- that the state thereafter stopped treating religious objectors as a problem.

The chapter must preserve continued dissent and the later trajectory of pacifist believers/groups.
