## 0. Chapter thesis

The chapter must not say simply:

> `В 1929 году советская власть запретила религию.`

That is legally inaccurate and historically too weak.

The better documentary thesis is:

> **The 8 April 1929 decree preserved registered local worship, but legally compressed the church toward a supervised cult association and cut away much of the social, educational, publishing, youth, charitable, congress, federal and itinerant-mission infrastructure that Russian Baptists had built during the preceding decade.**

The chapter therefore answers one concrete question:

> **What changed in the ordinary life of a Baptist congregation when the abstract language of religious liberty was translated into the 1929 legal regime?**

## 2. Registration: worship remains possible, but only through a supervised local association

### Articles 2–4

The act registered religious associations as either:
- religious societies;
- groups of believers.

A religious society was defined as a **local** association of at least 20 adult believers. Societies and groups had no legal-person status. They could begin activity only after registration.

This matters because the legal subject is not an autonomous nationwide church. It is a locally registered association whose existence and officers are visible to the administrative apparatus.

Safe production wording:
> `Закон не закрыл одним постановлением все молитвенные собрания. Он поставил легальную церковную жизнь в зависимость от регистрации местной общины и заранее лишил её полноценной юридической субъектности.`

Status: **PRIMARY CLOSED**.

## 3. A direct before/after map of Baptist church life

This is the analytical spine of the chapter.

| Baptist institutional life already documented in earlier chapters | 1929 legal mechanism | Effect to narrate |
|---|---|---|
| local congregation | arts.2–4 | registration becomes entry gate |
| prayer building | art.10 | one prayer room/building per association; property use contractually controlled |
| publishing/printing | art.11 | commercial/industrial contracts tied to printing religious literature explicitly outside permitted transactions |
| ordinary general meetings | art.12 | require administrative permission |
| elected local leaders | arts.13–14 | registrar can challenge/remove particular executive candidates |
| mutual aid / famine-relief style church welfare | art.17(a–b) | mutual-aid funds, material support and non-cult property use forbidden |
| children, youth, women, Bible circles, libraries | art.17(c) | specifically enumerated organized activities forbidden |
| theological education | art.18 | religious teaching excluded from ordinary educational institutions; special theological courses require special permission |
| travelling evangelists / multi-congregation preaching | art.19 | territorial scope of ministers/preachers restricted |
| local, regional, all-Russian/all-Union congresses | art.20 | each congress requires special permission |
| union/congress executive organs | arts.21–22 | they may exist, but their lists/materials go to the authority and they have no legal-person rights or central property/contract capacity |
| congress organization | art.24 | initiators/organizers are legally specified |
| prayer-house property | arts.25–29ff. | cult property is treated as nationalized state property in believers' use, with inventory and inspection obligations |

The point is not that every activity disappeared instantly on 8 April. The point is that the legal architecture made many previously normal Baptist institutions either forbidden, permit-dependent, territorially narrowed, or institutionally hollowed out.

Status: **PRIMARY LEGAL MAPPING CLOSED CORE**.

## 4. Prayer house — not abolition, but controlled use

### Article 10

Believers could receive special prayer buildings and cult objects for free use under contract and could rent other premises for prayer meetings.

But:
- each society/group could use only **one** prayer room;
- the arrangement was contractual and administrative;
- later property provisions treated the cult property as nationalized and subject to inventory/control.

Do not write:
> `После 8 апреля все молитвенные дома были автоматически национализированы впервые.`

Nationalization flows from the earlier 1918 separation regime and is operationalized here. Chapter 14 should explain the legal mechanics rather than invent a one-day confiscation event.

Status: **PRIMARY CLOSED**.

---

## 5. Printing and the institutional press

### Article 11

The law allowed transactions tightly connected with cult maintenance — caretaker, firewood, repairs, ritual supplies, rent of prayer premises.

But it expressly excluded contractual relations pursuing commercial/industrial purposes even when connected with religion; the text gives as an example **printing houses for religious-moral books**.

For the Baptist story this connects directly to:
- the publishing expansion documented in Chapters 7–9 and 12;
- the final 1929 issues of `Баптист`;
- loss of a normal union publishing infrastructure.

Important caution:
> art.11 by itself does not prove the administrative history of the closure of every Baptist periodical or printing house. The production chapter must pair the statute with periodical-specific closure evidence.

Status: **LEGAL MECHANISM CLOSED / EACH PRESS CLOSURE REQUIRES ITS OWN SOURCE**.

## 6. General congregational meetings

### Article 12

General meetings of religious societies/groups required permission from the relevant local authority.

This is stronger and more precise than saying merely `assemblies were controlled`.

It also requires a distinction:
- art.12 = **general meetings of the registered local religious society/group**;
- art.16 = meetings of executive/audit organs could occur without notification/permission;
- art.20 = **religious congresses/conferences** required special permission separately.

Do not merge these into one generic `all meetings needed permission` claim.

Status: **PRIMARY CLOSED**.

---

## 7. State veto over local executive personnel

### Articles 13–14

Local religious associations elected executive organs by open vote. But the registering authority received the right to reject particular persons from the executive body.

Analytical significance:
> church members still elected officers, but the state possessed an explicit personnel-screening power over the legal executive body.

Do not call this a statutory power to appoint the pastor directly. That is not what art.14 says.

Status: **PRIMARY CLOSED**.

## 8. Article 17 — the chapter's most consequential church-life provision

Article 17 forbade religious associations to:
- establish mutual-aid funds, cooperatives and production associations;
- use property for purposes other than religious needs;
- provide material support to their members;
- organize special children’s, youth, women’s, prayer and other meetings;
- organize general Bible, literary, handicraft, labor, religious-instruction and similar meetings/groups/circles/departments;
- arrange excursions and children’s playgrounds;
- open libraries and reading rooms;
- organize sanatoria and medical aid.

Inside prayer buildings only books necessary for the given cult could be stored.

This provision should be shown against what the Baptists had just been doing:
- famine relief and mutual aid;
- youth congresses and youth work;
- Bible education;
- libraries/periodicals;
- organized evangelistic and educational networks.

Do not overstate:
> `The Bible itself was banned.` — **REJECTED**.

The law targeted organized Bible meetings/circles and tightly restricted what books could be stored in prayer premises. Individual possession/reading is a different claim.

Status: **PRIMARY CLOSED / HIGH NARRATIVE VALUE**.

---

## 9. Article 18 — religious education

The original 1929 wording barred teaching religious doctrines in state, public and private educational/upbringing institutions.

Special theological courses could be opened only with special state permission.

For Baptists, this must be paired with the already documented Bible-course trajectory and the March 1929 closure around P. V. Ivanov-Klyshnikov.

Critical chronology:
- if the course closure occurred on **3 March 1929**, it predates the 8 April act;
- therefore the chapter must not causally attribute that March closure to a law that had not yet been adopted;
- instead it demonstrates that the statute formalized/reinforced a tightening already underway.

Status: **PRIMARY LEGAL RULE CLOSED / COURSE EVENT SOURCE CONTROL SEPARATE**.

## 10. Article 19 — itinerant ministry territorially narrowed

Correct original numbering:
> **Article 19**, not 24.

The activity area of ministers, religious preachers and mentors was tied to:
- the residence of members of the association they served;
- the location of the relevant prayer room.

Those permanently serving several associations were restricted to the territory where those believers lived.

This provision directly attacks one of the structural strengths of the pre-1929 Baptist movement: travelling evangelists and inter-congregational itinerant ministry.

Do not convert the territorial rule into a claim that every trip by a preacher was a criminal offense. Enforcement and sanctions require separate evidence.

Status: **PRIMARY CLOSED**.

---

## 11. Articles 20–24 — the federation remains on paper, but its operating space is constricted

### Article 20 — congress permission
Local, all-Russian and all-Union religious congresses/meetings required special permission in each case from the designated state authority.

### Article 21 — congress executive
A congress could elect an executive organ, but its member list and congress materials had to be supplied to the authority that permitted the congress.

### Article 22 — no legal-person capacity
Religious congresses and their executive organs lacked legal-person rights and were forbidden, among other things, to:
- maintain central donation funds;
- impose compulsory collections;
- possess/acquire/rent cult property;
- enter contracts/transactions.

### Article 23 — seals/stamps only for religious business
Executive organs could use seals/stamps/forms only for religious matters.

### Article 24 — who may initiate congresses
Religious societies/groups and their executive organs, as well as congress executive organs, were defined as permissible initiators/organizers.

Editorial synthesis:
> `The law did not erase the word “congress”; it deprived nationwide church organization of much of the legal, financial and property capacity required to function as a normal federation.`

Status: **PRIMARY CLOSED**.

## 12. Property — avoid a false “1929 invented nationalization” story

### Articles 25–29 and following

Cult property is described as nationalized, registered by local authorities and placed in believers’ use. The users assumed maintenance obligations and had to allow inspection.

This is continuity with the 1918 legal premise, now embedded in a much more detailed control regime.

Safe framing:
> `1929 did not invent state ownership of cult property; it integrated that earlier principle into a denser registration, contract, inventory and inspection system.`

Status: **PRIMARY CLOSED CORE**.

## 13. 18 May 1929 constitutional amendment — wording correction

The XIV All-Russian Congress of Soviets changed article 4 of the RSFSR Constitution on **18 May 1929**.

The exact 1929 formula was:
> `свобода религиозных исповеданий и антирелигиозной пропаганды`.

It replaced the earlier constitutional formula that protected:
> `свобода религиозной и антирелигиозной пропаганды`.

Important correction:
- do **not** quote the 1929 amendment as `свобода отправления религиозных культов`;
- that language belongs to later constitutional formulations, especially the 1936 USSR Constitution tradition;
- the 1929 amendment specifically removes **religious propaganda** from the paired constitutional guarantee while retaining **anti-religious propaganda**.

This is not semantic trivia. It directly matters to Baptist evangelism/missions.

Status: **PRIMARY WORDING CLOSED / ORIGINAL PAGE IMAGE RECEIPT OPEN**.
